Title: Learning How to Write a Thesis
Tags: Workflow, Data Science


I think by far writing a thesis has been the hardest thing I've ever done.
It's been harder than getting a bachelors, harder than getting N2 in Japanese.

The hard part isn't the content, or the work. It's the motivation, the consistency. Continuing even when you might be the only one of your peers still in your program, or the topic has no more interesting things to peak your curiousity, or the project itself seems like it could never be useful to anyone.

-----------

It's grindy work. And it's not short. It has taken me at least a hundred hours or more, maybe even 300 I don't know. All while working, socializing, and having every other kind of distraction come up.
You can't do it in a day, and you can't do it in a week. And I doubt a month is feasible either.

The hardest part is just bringing your focus to the thesis. Because no one will cause you to focus on it. You will be alone.

Your professor is there to check in. That's your weekly pillar of strength. Depending on circumstance, that may be the single person in the world who cares what you are working on. But you'll still be on your own to get it all done. And they may never know the details even half as well as you until the end, so it can still be hard to relate with them. But relating will help you write better.

-----------

Your first goal is to figure out what your goal is.
After that, it's to figure out if it's even reasonable. Your professor can help with this part.
After that, you work on the goal you set. This is the familiar part, the part that a bachelor's prepared you for.
While doing so, be sure to document everything as best as possible.

Eventually, you need to reach some place that you decide is the end. There will always be more to do, and the project will never be finished, so you and your advisor need to decide together what counts as "enough".

When you're close to that "enough" point. You can write down everything in a giant essay. 
After that you make a presentation, 
Then a panel of judges made up people you are familiar with (and thus you will care deeply about what they think of you) come to decide whether it truly was "enough".

These are all difficult tasks in their own right. But for me, I struggled with it a lot. Writing was always something I struggled with when I was young. Not because I couldn't write, but because the task itself was so vague. My perfectionism would take over, and I would be unable to write a single word. I could never decide on the "correct" word, or what "correct" even was, so I would end class periods with a blank piece of paper.
Being assigned a 4-24 month project where I don't even know what I'm supposed to do would probably have been kid-me's worst nightmare. And while I have struggled a lot with it, I think doing a thesis has helped me refine and build habits to get independent work done in the face of intense vagueness.

-----------
I have learned workflow techniques to write long-form content. And while I wouldn't consider myself a writer, the task of writing a 100 page essay or even a book sounds surprisingly approachable now.

What I learned about writing:
- I learned that I could label parts of my text with highlighting to mark which pieces to focus on. This saves tons of time scanning the text, which grows more impossible to scan by the day.
- I learned that making a table of contents early allows you to create hyperlinks in your document to quickly get to relevant pieces you want to work on. And also helps you stay organized.
- It is better to just brain dump all of what you want to write in casual prose, because you can change it later to make it a more formal prose. This helps reduce stress from perfectionism.

In addition to the writing portion, I learned about how to be a better data scientist too.

What I learned about data science:
- Deciding on a baseline is a great thing to do. But make *absolutely* sure that every single part of it is double checked and polished before moving on.
    - make evaluation code that prints all relevant information and saves it.
    - make sure your evaluation data is, actually, in fact, valid.
- Training models is a lot of fun. But the best models will never give good results if your data is unclean and poorly formatted. Focusing on the data itself can lead to just as good of results, if not better! Specifically analyzing it, cleaning it, getting more data, etc.

I also learned a bit of how to do better research and literature reviews.

What I learned about research:
- Organizing research in a spreadsheet can be useful. Extracting common techniques used between papers becomes easier this way. You can make the decision process of what to do next in a project into a data-driven process by doing this. 
- If you extract out the techniques within papers into a spreadsheet, then it becomes obvious what is shared. Then you can reorganize these shared techniques into a second spreadsheet where the rows are the different techniques, and then evaluate what is viable for you to implement.
    - in other words: read paper -> which_papers_did_what_techniques.csv -> which_techniques_were_frequently_used.csv

- I also learned that when researching, it is very helpful to just consult an expert. I was worried I would be inconveniencing people by asking them questions, but I found that often times people are quite happy to talk and share about their subject. After all, this is the subject that they have spent so long studying, so it's a great experience to be able to share that. I'm glad I got to meet with the Kaggle organizer. I wish I had reached out sooner!

-----------

As I write this, I still have to finalize my draft, and present my thesis. I'm not out of the woods yet, but I feel like I've come a long ways and wanted to write up my experience before I move on from this chapter of my life.
Doing a thesis has been a huge learning experience for me, and I'm glad to have done it.
